\subsection{Cooperation levels}

Average contribution intensity is moderately positive in both replications, and SI and SFI means remain nearly identical within each society (Table~\ref{tab:coop_summary}).
Run~B sits above Run~A for most of the horizon (Figure~\ref{fig:prop_cross}).
Round-mean series are only weakly aligned across replications, and neither series shows a significant Mann--Kendall trend.
Medians stay below means, and zero-contribution shares remain higher in SFI than in SI.
Matched institution means are descriptive under forced assignment rather than evidence of a causal institution effect.

Early-round composition differs.
In Run~A, most SFI agents contribute nothing in round~1, then escalate once peer history appears (Figure~\ref{fig:zeros}).
In Run~B, that opening freeze is largely absent.
Both societies still settle into matched institution means after the opening window.

\subsection{Social pressure and subsequent intensity}

After gossip and bad-reputation events, mean next-round intensity falls among SFI agents in both replications (Table~\ref{tab:event_study}; Figure~\ref{fig:rep_cross}).
Gossip associations are larger in magnitude than bad-reputation associations in SFI.
Reputation-drop associations in SFI remain negative, though attenuated in Run~B.

SI responses reverse sign across replications.
In Run~A, SI means after social marks are negative; in Run~B they are positive, including a large positive gossip association.
SI and SFI agents therefore behave differently under the same social-information stack.
A universal claim that gossip lowers intensity for all agents is unsupported.

\subsection{Rationales after social events}

Post-event rationales rarely describe reputation repair (Table~\ref{tab:motifs}).
In Run~A ($N{=}114$), opportunistic framing appears 29 times and reputation-management language only 3 times whereas explicit gossip references appear 0 times.
Inter-coder agreement on a stratified subsample is substantial (percent agreement 0.83; Cohen's $\kappa{=}0.72$ for primary labels).
Agents more often cite weak marginal returns than restored standing.
Cross-replication keyness contrasts show lexical differences: SI texts emphasize strategy and self-interest; SFI texts emphasize incentives and immediacy.
Fairness language remains rare in contribution talk.

\subsection{Shocks, democracy, and redistribution}

Shock-window mean intensity changes are statistically non-significant by Wilcoxon tests, yet composition moves.
Run~A shows a sharp SI zero spike after the first shock, with payoff-maximizing rationales despite large remaining wealth.
Mean intensity recovers within one or two rounds.
Shock and democracy rounds coincide, so climate damage and rule politics are entangled.

Democracy expands subsidies and equity themes in both replications, but the literal rule path diverges (Table~\ref{tab:democracy}).
Only Run~B weakens punishment.
Proposal rhetoric is portable cooperation boilerplate.

Shock coverage equals about three-quarters in both replications, while terminal pools dwarf cumulative payouts and wealth gaps widen (Figures~\ref{fig:ldf_cross} and~\ref{fig:wealth_cross}).
High coverage does not imply stock equalization.
Agents cannot observe the numeric pool balance, so fund-stock optimization is an implausible behavioral account.
