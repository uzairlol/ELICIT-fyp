\subsection{Collective action, sanctions, and reputation}

Collective-action problems arise when private incentives erode shared returns \pcite{olson1965logic,hardin1968tragedy}.
Laboratory and evolutionary traditions show that monitoring, costly punishment, and reputation can sustain cooperation \pcite{fehr2002altruistic,boyd2003evolution,axelrod1981evolution,axelrod1984evolution}.
Indirect reciprocity and image scoring formalize how observed giving shapes future partners \pcite{wedekind2000cooperation,panchanathan2004indirect}.
Milinski and colleagues argue that reputation helps solve commons dilemmas by linking contribution to standing \pcite{milinski2002reputation}.
Nowak lists reputation among the core evolutionary routes to cooperation \pcite{nowak2006five}.
Across these lines, the working hypothesis is directional.
More social visibility should raise, or at least protect, cooperative transfers.

Institutional design complements reputation.
Ostrom documents polycentric rules, graduated sanctions, and participatory governance in durable commons regimes \pcite{ostrom1990governing}.
Those design principles motivate dual channels in computational societies.
Agents may face formal enforcement inside one institution and social evaluation everywhere.
They may also revise fiscal parameters by vote.
The classical literatures rarely ask what happens when social marks land on agents who cannot exit and cannot punish peers.

\subsection{Language-model agents as strategic laboratories}

Language-model agents change the measurement surface of social dilemmas.
They produce numeric choices together with natural-language rationales, belief updates, and peer judgments \pcite{park2023generative,sumers2024cognitive}.
Game-theoretic LLM platforms treat such agents as laboratories for strategic interaction \pcite{mao2023alympics}.
Cooperation benchmarks report heterogeneous success across games, prompts, and communication regimes \pcite{piatti2024govsim,madmoun2025communication,liang2025everyone}.
Some societies sustain contributions when talk is rich.
Others collapse once incentives dominate.

A growing strand shows that explicit reasoning can reduce cooperation.
Models that deliberate more intensely often favor individually rational free-riding \pcite{li2025spontaneous,freeriders2025}.
That pattern mirrors human ``spontaneous giving and calculated greed'' and warns against equating fluent justification with prosocial commitment.
Norm-focused multi-agent studies likewise report partial emergence and fragile stability rather than automatic internalization \pcite{gupta2025norms,ashery2025conventions,cross2025norms}.
These results motivate separating average transfer levels from norm strength.

\subsection{Reputation systems as assumed stabilizers}

Several recent systems treat reputation networks or peer talk as cooperation technology.
RepuNet-style architectures encode standing so agents can condition on peer history \pcite{repunet2025}.
GovSim and related communication protocols emphasize information sharing as a coordination route \pcite{piatti2024govsim,madmoun2025communication}.
Punishment-strategy transplants from classical evolutionary models show that explicit sanctions can organize behavior when costs and targets are clear \pcite{warnakulasuriya2025punishment}.
The cumulative message is optimistic.
If agents can talk and score one another, collective outcomes should improve.

That optimism is incomplete for climate asymmetry.
Where gossip substitutes for formal punishment, the mapping from stigma to repair is unsettled.
Self-reinforcing salience loops in LLM inference can lock opportunistic frames once they appear \pcite{howlround2025}.
Identification of free-riders without sanction reach is a known failure mode in mixed populations.
Social monitoring can then name agents without raising contributions.
Prior LLM work rarely event-studies contribution intensity after negative social marks under forced institutional partitions.

\subsection{Climate risk sharing and computational governance}

Climate governance couples mitigation public goods with asymmetric vulnerability and compensation claims.
Loss-and-damage instruments are dual-use in spirit.
Contributions can fund shared returns and contingent payouts after shocks.
Computational societies that combine those fiscal rules with LLM agents that gossip and vote remain rare.
Endogenous democracy lets agents revise punishment, subsidy, and equity weights.
That channel is politically meaningful.
It also confounds clean shock identification when votes coincide with damage rounds.

Inequality complicates interpretation of social pressure \pcite{lu2025aligning,ashery2025conventions}.
If stigma falls on low-capacity agents without Stage-2 tools, withdrawal may be a constrained response.
Shock-free free-choice baselines in related work show that reputation can coordinate sorting when exit is available.
They also show identification of defectors without cross-institution sanctioning power.
Those boundary results define when reputation helps versus when it coincides with withdrawal.

\subsection{Research gap}

Prior work largely assumes that reputation and gossip raise cooperation, or at least do not systematically reduce it.
LLM-agent papers that celebrate communication rarely measure post-stigma intensity under climate asymmetry across independent replications.
Climate-finance simulations rarely instrument social stigma with pairwise consistency scoring and gossip bulletins.
The gap is therefore joint.
We need an environment where social marks, forced institutions, shocks, and redistribution coexist, and where multi-seed evidence distinguishes robust associations from seed-specific paths.
The present study addresses that gap with a dual-run event study of contribution intensity after reputation and gossip events.
