The environment is designed to place social pressure where classical theory expects it to help, while removing exit and equalizing enforcement rights that would otherwise confound the test.
Agents inhabit a repeated public-goods economy with developed and developing roles that govern wealth, vulnerability, and institutional assignment.
Each round, agents choose Stage-1 contributions inside their assigned institution.
Only members of the enforcing institution then allocate Stage-2 sanctions and rewards.
Climatic shocks, fund accounting, social scoring, and democracy occur on a fixed schedule described in Section~4.

\subsection{Why two institutions}

Cooperation problems often mix formal sanctions with informal evaluation.
We therefore run two institutions in parallel.
The enforcing institution (SI) combines Stage-1 public-goods returns with peer punishment and reward, framed to agents as a binding treaty.
The non-enforcing institution (SFI) provides Stage-1 returns without Stage-2 sanctioning, framed as a non-binding agreement.
In the climate package, assignment is forced every round: developed agents enter SI and developing agents enter SFI.
The partition creates asymmetric sanction reach while holding the social-information stack common.
Free institutional choice is reserved for boundary baselines discussed later.

\subsection{Stage-1 public good and contribution intensity}

Let $c_i$ denote agent $i$'s Stage-1 contribution and let $G$ denote agents in the same institution.
With multiplier $m$ and group size $n=|G|$, each member receives
\[
\mathrm{share}_i=\frac{m\cdot C}{n},\qquad C=\sum_{j\in G}c_j.
\]
The marginal per-capita return is $m/n$.
Contribution budgets equal current wealth, floored at zero.
Decision cards state budget bounds, group size, marginal return, and recent same-institution averages.

Absolute contributions differ by orders of magnitude across groups.
We therefore analyze contribution intensity
\[
\mathrm{prop}_{i,t}=\frac{c_{i,t}}{w_{i,t}},
\]
where $w_{i,t}$ is end-of-round wealth.
All event studies use changes in $\mathrm{prop}$ so that social marks are comparable across wealth scales.

\subsection{Stage-2 enforcement as a second-order public good}

Only SI members enter Stage-2, with a sanction budget proportional to wealth.
Punishment tokens reduce a target's payoff at a fixed effect-to-cost ratio; reward tokens raise payoffs at unit cost.
Enforcement is costly to the sender, so provision of sanctions is itself a second-order public good.
SFI members skip Stage-2 entirely.
We track sanction burden as context for interpretation rather than as the primary estimand.

\subsection{Dual-use loss-and-damage fund}

Climate risk sharing requires a redistributive instrument that is not identical to the public good.
Stage-1 contributions therefore act as dual-use deposits: each contributed unit also enters a collective loss-and-damage pool.
Agents are told that contributions support both emissions-reduction framing and disaster payouts.
They do not observe the live pool balance.
After climatic shocks, developing agents may receive payouts subject to coverage caps and equity weights.
Coverage is the ratio of aggregate payouts to aggregate gross damage in a shock round.
High coverage can coexist with widening wealth gaps when collection and growth dominate disbursement.

\subsection{Social information without mechanical force}

Three linked channels create social pressure without forcing higher contributions.
After contributions, evaluators score targets on a one-to-ten consistency scale.
Agents observe an aggregate peer-trust rating derived from those scores.
Bad reputation is defined as an aggregate score below four; a reputation drop is a decline of at least one point between rounds.
Low pairwise scores generate gossip items injected into subsequent prompts, naming the observer, target, score, and a short comment.
Capacity limits keep only a top set of items.
Gossip targeting in the event study is reconstructed from eligibility conditions consistent with bulletin generation.
Any reputation repair must arise from prompted choice.

\subsection{Democracy as inexpensive meta-governance}

Every five rounds, agents may propose and vote on a bounded whitelist of parameters, including subsidy fraction, punishment and reward effects, and loss-and-damage equity or damage weights.
Majority outcomes update the live rule set.
Democracy lets agents rewrite payoff consequences of enforcement and redistribution without paying Stage-2 costs.
We analyze adopted rules as environmental context for contribution dynamics.

\subsection{Agent interface}

Agents are prompted language models with a default self-interested persona and soft climate-role guidance.
Outputs include contribution amounts, sanction maps, proposals, votes, and rationales.
Belief states update after rounds with trust labels.
Fluent rationales are evidence of stated reasoning, not proof of internalized norms.
