Reputation and gossip are widely treated as stabilizers of cooperation in multi-agent societies.
We test that assumption for large language model (LLM) agents in a climate-risk public-goods economy with asymmetric vulnerability and a dual-use loss-and-damage pool.
Two independent full-protocol runs, identical except for the random seed, each place twenty-six agents in thirty rounds under forced institutional assignment, climatic shocks, democratic parameter updating, peer consistency scoring, aggregate reputation, and gossip bulletins.
The primary outcome is contribution intensity relative to wealth.
Across both runs, agents in the non-enforcing institution reduce intensity after bad-reputation and gossip events on average.
Responses among enforcing-institution agents reverse sign across seeds.
Average cooperation remains moderately positive in both societies, yet path stability is limited and democratic adaptation is path dependent.
Shock damage coverage is high while developed--developing wealth gaps widen.
The robust pattern is institutional heterogeneity in social pressure.
Social monitoring associates with withdrawal among developing agents without Stage-2 enforcement, rather than with universal reputation repair.
