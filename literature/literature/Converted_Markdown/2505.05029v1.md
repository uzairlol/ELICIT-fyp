## Abstract

The tragedy of the commons, where individual self-interest leads to collectively disastrous outcomes, is a pervasive challenge in human society. Recent studies have demonstrated that similar phenomena can arise in generative multi-agent systems (MASs). To address this challenge, this paper explores the use of reputation systems as a remedy. We propose RepuNet, a dynamic, dual-level reputation framework that models both agent-level reputation dynamics and system-level network evolution. Specifically, driven by direct interactions and indirect gossip, agents form reputations for both themselves and their peers, and decide whether to connect or disconnect other agents for future interactions. Through two distinct scenarios, we show that RepuNet effectively mitigates the 'tragedy of the commons', promoting and sustaining cooperation in generative MASs. Moreover, we find that repuation systems can give rise to rich emergent behaviors in generative MASs, such as the formation of cooperative clusters, the social isolation of exploitative agents, and the preference for sharing positive gossip rather than negative ones.

## 1 Introduction

Imagine that you and your neighbors share a small pond stocked with fish. Initially, everyone takes only what they need, but then one person decides to take a little extra, reasoning that, after all, the pond is full of fish. Word spreads, others follow, and soon the once-thriving pond is nearly empty. This is the tragedy of the commons— the social dilemma where individual self-interest leads to the disastrous collective outcome of cooperation collapse [20, 32]. This dilemma extends far beyond fishing and underpins a wide array of challenges, such as resource allocation [34], climate change [11], and self-driving coordination [9], crucial to both human society and AI systems.

Recent studies on large language model (LLM)-based agents, often referred to as generative agents, have revealed the existence of the 'tragedy of the commons' in generative multi-agent systems (MASs). For example, in resource allocation tasks, Piatti et al. [34] observed that generative agents tended to over-exploit shared resources, resulting in rapid depletion and long-term unsustainability. Similarly, in iterated Prisoner's Dilemma settings, agents often responded to a single defection with irreversible retaliation, leading to cascading mutual defection and the breakdown of cooperation [1, 46, 15]. While these studies have documented the widespread 'tragedy of the commons', effective solutions remain elusive.

This paper explores the use of reputation systems as a remedy. Reputation is a set of collective beliefs or evaluations about individuals based on their past behavior, enabling agents to form expectations about others [5, 43, 30]. As an emergent social signal, it encodes perception of trustworthiness, reliability, and integrity. A reputation system operationalizes this signal by systematically collecting, aggregating, and disseminating reputational information across agents. Proven effective in both human societies and traditional MASs, reputation systems offer a fesible solution to fostering cooperation even among strangers, evoking social norms, and serving as lightweight, scalable substitutes for direct monitoring or formal enforcement [44, 3, 17].

In this paper, we introduce RepuNet, the first reputation system designed for generative MASs. In RepuNet, every interaction is rooted through an explicit network. It operates on two levels: agent-level reputation dynamics with system-level network evolution. Specifically, at the agent level, echoing real-world reputation dynamics [39, 19, 17], RepuNet enables agents shape initial self- and peer-reputations after their first encounter and iteratively update them after each subsequent interaction. Besides, gossip has been a key mechanism for spreading reputational information across networks throughout human evolution. Inspired by this, agents evaluate each direct encounter and, when (dis)satisfied, broadcast that information, updating the target's reputation. In RepuNet, we explicitly couple reputation updates to topology changes: once a reputation is revised, it drives network rewiring. At the system level, agents strengthen ties to high-reputation partners and sever links to disreputable ones, reflecting the human tendency to seek trustworthy collaborators and abandon exploitative relationships. This network rewiring reshapes future encounter probabilities and fosters long-term cooperation among reputable agents. An overview of our reputation system is provided in Figure 1.

In the experiment, we consider two scenarios: (i) voluntary participation, adapted from human field experiments [53], models a collective resource-sharing dilemma; and (ii) trading investment, derived from an economic game [4], captures a sequential investment dilemma. Within generative MASs, our experiments replicate the 'tragedy of the commons' previously observed in human studies: cooperation collapses in both scenarios when agents act without RepuNet, prioritising individual gain and driving collective exploitation. In contrast, with RepuNet, agents are effectively incentivized to sustain cooperation, with most agents prioritizing collective rationality to avoid the 'tragedy of the commons'. Moreover, our experiments reveal rich emergent phenomena: (i) cooperative agents with high reputations self-organise into tightly connected clusters that persist over time; (ii) these clusters selectively isolate those low-reputation agents who exhibit defective behaviors; (iii) unlike humans, generative agents prefer to share positive gossip rather than negative ones; and (iv) without RepuNet, dishonesty leads to the collapse of networks. Ablation study further confirms each component's effectiveness: removing any single component lowers cooperation rates and can even trigger a complete collapse.

In summary, our key contributions are as follows:

1. We replicate the 'tragedy of the commons' phenomenon, previously observed in human studies [53], within generative MASs, confirming its widespread prevalence and emphasizing the critical need to address this challenge.
2. We introduce RepuNet, the first reputation system for generative MASs, which not only operationalizes

the reputation dynamics through direct social encounters and indirect gossip but also shapes network evolution, thereby effectively avoding the 'tragedy of the commons'.

3. We show RepuNet induces various emergent phenomena, such as cooperative clustering, the isolation of exploitative agents, and a bias toward positive gossip, demonstrating the crucial role of reputation systems in catalyzing complex collective behaviors in generative MASs.

## 2 Related Work

The Existence of the Tragedy of the Commons in Generative Agents. Recent studies have explicitly identified the presence of the tragedy in generative MASs [34, 29, 26, 1, 36]. Mozikov et al. [29] examined how simulated emotions affect generative agents' behavior in social dilemmas, finding that even positive emotions can unpredictably reduce cooperation among generative agents. Piatti et al. [34] proposed a simulation platform to study resource-sharing scenarios, revealing that agents frequently over-exploited shared natural resources, thereby failing to maintain sustainable cooperation and exacerbating the 'tragedy of the commons'. Mao et al. [26] showed that, in a multi-round water-auction game, a lower initial resource-supply ratio provokes generative agents to bid more aggressively, reducing overall survival rates. Akata et al. [1] found that, in iterated two-strategy games, these generative agents often permanently defect following a single betrayal, hindering long-term cooperation. While these studies highlight the problem, they primarily focus on analyzing generative agents' performance rather than proposing solutions to avoid the 'tragedy of the commons', which remains a pressing open challenge in generative MASs.

Reputation for Traditional Multi-agent Systems. Reputation, defined as the opinions or evaluations that agents form about one another [41, 24], has drawn significant interest across various fields [35, 40]. Extensive research in MASs has explored how reputation facilitates cooperation to avoid the 'tragedy of the commons' [31, 42, 35]. However, these systems are typically rule-based or learning-based [14, 35, 19, 2]. For example, Anastassacos et al. [2] demonstrated that reputation fosters trust and cooperation among reinforcement learning (RL)-based agents. Fu et al. [14] found that cooperation can prevail when rule-based agents are able to alter their partnership based on reputation. While these studies offer valuable insights, they cannot provide a direct solution, as they have historically failed to leverage the power of LLMs.

Social Networks in Generative Multi-Agent Systems. Social networks profoundly influence generative MASs by shaping agent interactions, information dissemination, and behavioral coordination [45]. Recent studies have leveraged generative agents to simulate complex dynamics within social networks, aiming to explore emergent human-like behaviors [16, 47, 52, 8]. For instance, Gao et al. [16] introduced the S3 framework, which employs generative agents to replicate nuanced individual and collective human behaviors—including emotions, attitudes, and interactions—within real-world social networks. Yang et al. [52] developed the OASIS framework, a simulated social media network capable of scaling to millions of agents, facilitating extensive investigations into collective phenomena such as viral information diffusion, echo chamber formation, and group polarization dynamics. Although these studies highlight significant advancements in generative MASs for capturing emergent group behaviors, the fundamental challenge of maintaining cooperation within dynamically evolving social network structures—an essential factor significantly impacting agent interactions—remains underexplored.

Overall, the current literature has not attempted to develop a reputation system specially for generative MASs to foster and sustain cooperation in avoiding the 'tragedy of the commons'.

## 3 Reputation-based Multi-agent System

In this section, we first formally describe the 'tragedy of the commons'. Then in Section 3.2, we outline the formalization of our reputation-based MAS. After providing an overview of the reputation-based MAS and existing problem, we introduce RepuNet from two perspectives: (i) reputation dynamics at the agent level and (ii) network dynamics at the system level. ## 3.1 The Tragedy of the Commons

While the optimal group outcome occurs when everyone contributes fully, each individual can improve their own payoff by contributing less and free-riding on others. If too many players free-ride, the public good diminishes significantly, leaving everyone worse off, thus creating the 'tragedy of the commons'. Note that we model this through binary choices in n-player games, real-world commons dilemmas typically involve additional complexities beyond this fundamental model.

## 3.2 Formalization

In a MAS, interactions between agents can be represented by a network, which determines the likelihood of whom they meet and interact with. Let an ordered pair G = (V, E) denote a directed graph, where V is the set of vertices representing the agents in the system, and E ⊆ {(i, j)|i, j ∈ V 2 and i ̸= j} is the set of directed edges, each of which is an ordered pair of vertices. A directed edge from vertex i to vertex j indicates that agent j is reachable from agent i, meaning that agent j is viewed as a potential partner that agent i is willing to interact in future interactions.

In our system, reputation arises from information exchange within the embedded network. Specifically, two types of reputations can emerge in our reputation-based MAS: (i) self-reputation, which reflects an agent's selfperception of how others evaluate it based on past interactions [17], and (ii) peer-reputation, which captures an agent's evaluation of other's behaviors in previous interactions [5, 45]. Regardless of the types, formally, reputation in our system can be represented as a standardized quintuple: r = ⟨a, s, o, c, µ⟩. Here, a denotes the basic information of the agent being evaluated (i.e., the evaluated target), such as its ID and name. Since reputation is context-dependent (e.g., one may be a good parent but not a good colleague) [38], s and o denote the scenario and role of the evaluated target, respectively. c represents the reputation in natural language, e.g., "James is too self-centered, often prioritizing his interests over the community's needs"; µ quantifies the reputation described in c, ranging from -1 to 1, with higher scores indicating a better reputation. We consider that for each agent, the self-reputation and peer-reputation are stored in its RepuNet database, denoted as R . Note that an agent's reputation always refers to the most recently stored value in the database, as reputations are dynamically updated.

To allow RepuNet to be integrated with other MASs involving agent interactions, such as Generative Agents [33] and ProAgents [54], we consider it as an event-driven system that responds to agent encounters. Driven by direct encounters and indirect gossip, our reputation-based MAS dynamically update reputations in response to new interactions, allowing evaluations to adapt to evolving agent behaviors. We model these dynamics on two levels: reputation dynamics at the agent level and network dynamics at the system level, which will be discussed in following sections.

## 3.3 Reputation Dynamics at the Agent Level

This section explores how reputations are shaped and updated through direct encounters and indirect gossip [31, 50, 17].

## 3.3.1 Reputation Driven by Direct Encounters

In the real world, reputation is often shaped by direct social encounters, such as when a seller provides quality products [39, 19]. Reputation is not fixed or unchanging; rather, it evolves over time based on new encounters and the cumulative evaluations of one's past behavior, that is, one's previous reputation [17]. Inspired by these, in our system, agents can form both self-reputation and peer-reputation based on their first social encounter. Over time, as agents meet and interact again, they update these reputations by considering both previous reputations and the impressions formed during the latest interaction.

Specifically, let mij (t) represent the direct encounter between agent i and j at time t. After they first encounter, for instance, the agent i generates a peer-reputation for the agent j, denoted as ri →j (t). As time evolves, on subsequent encounters, agent i updates agent j's reputation based on the new encounter mij (t + 1) and previous peer-reputation ri →j (t). We represent this process by a LLM-based operation ri →j (t + 1) ← ShapeRepuPeer(mij (t + 1), ri →j (t) , if r i→j (t) ̸= ϕ ). By shaping peer-reputation, we expect agents are able to select partners rather than relying on random encounters, avoiding free-riders and fostering cooperation in generative MASs.

In addition to peer-reputation, agents can also generate self-reputations to reflect their self-perceptions. To ensure alignment with their personalities, generative agents are prompted to generate self-reputations based on both their agent descriptions D and the direct encounter mij (t). Similar to shaping peer-reputation, as time evolves, once agent i encounters another agent x, not necessarily the previous agent j, we instruct agent i to update its self-reputation based on the new encounter mix(t + 1) and its previous self-reputation ri(t). The process can be represented by the operation ri(t+1) ← ShapeRepuSelf(Di, mix(t+1), ri(t) , if r i (t) ̸= ϕ ) . By shaping self-reputation, we aim to help agents maintain appropriate behaviors to uphold good reputations for cooperation.

## 3.3.2 Reputation Driven by Indirect Gossip

Gossip has been a part of human interactions throughout evolutionary history, from early hunter-gatherer societies to today's social media-driven culture [25, 48]. It involves the exchange of positive or negative information between a gossiper and a listener about an absent individual, making it an effective way to shape and spread reputations [13, 18]. In human societies, people have a strong tendency to gossip about others when they are (dis)satisfied with interactions, using it to influence others' reputations [13, 50]. Inspired by this, our system enables each agent to evaluate its satisfaction with others' behavior based on their innate attributes described in agent descriptions and then decide whether to gossip after each interaction. Due to the lack of space, details of the gossip process and related prompts are provided in Appendix D.

When gossip occurs, a listener generates a peer-reputation for the gossiped agent based on the gossip information (i.e., what the gossiper conveys). Let θ y (t) denote the gossip information about the gossiped agent y at the time t. The listener l shape a peer-reputation of agent y, denoted as rl →y (t). As time evolves, when agent l hears new gossip about agent y at time t + 1, we instruct agent l to update peer-reputation by considering both the latest gossip information θ y (t + 1) and the previous peer-reputation rl →y (t). The operation can be represented by rl →y (t + 1) ← ShapeRepuGossip(θ y (t + 1), rl →y (t) , if r l→y (t) ̸= ϕ). Through gossip, we aim to empower agents to exchange reputations of unknown agents, helping them assess potential partners and reduce defection risks.

## 3.4 Network Dynamics at the System Level

This section focuses on the system level, examining how networks evolve in RepuNet. Once an agent shape peer-reputations, it is natural to consider whether to build or maintain edges with them for future interactions based on their reputations. As RepuNet dynamics are event-driven, we analyze network evolution via direct encounters and indirect gossip.

## 3.4.1 Network Driven by Direct Interactions

In both human society and AI systems, social networks determine the likelihood of individuals meeting, interacting, and exchanging information [23, 45, 22]. Individuals seeking new partners prefer to build relationships with reputable agents and avoid exploitation by unilaterally ending unfavorable relationships [6, 12]. Inspired by these, we instruct each LLM-based agent to decide whether to build or maintain an edge with another to enable future opportunities for interaction. Specifically, let Di represent agent i's agent description, and mij (t) denote the direct encounter between agent i and j at time t. Once agent i shapes peer-reputation ri →j (t) of agent j, it considers whether to build or maintain the edge with agent j for future interactions. We represent this LLM-based operation by wi →j (t) ∈ {"Y",N"} ← InteractEdgeShape(Di, mij (t), ri →j (t)). Once the edge is formed (i.e., wi →j (t) = "Y"), agent i records an ordered pair (i, j) in its RepuNet database Ri. As the network evolves, we expect agents with high reputations form more edges and cluster together, fostering trustworthy relationships that sustain cooperation. Meanwhile, agents with low reputations form fewer edges and may become isolated from cooperative agents.

## 3.4.2 Network Driven by Indirect Gossip

Through indirect gossip, agents (as listeners) can shape the reputations of gossiped agents they have never met. However, they cannot build new edges with these agents, as no direct encounter has occurred. If the listener and the gossiped agent have previously interacted, the listener can reconsider whether to maintain the edge after updating the peer-reputation of the gossiped agent. Specifically, let Dl represent the listener l's agent description, θ y (t) represent the gossip information about the agent y at time t, and rl →y (t) denote the listener l shapes a peer-reputation of agent y. We instruct generative agents to consider whether to maintain an edge with the gossiped agent based on the aforementioned information, which can be formally represented by the operation wl →y (t) ∈ {"Y",N"} ← GossipEdgeShape(Dl, θ y (t), rl →y (t)). Once the edge is disconnect (i.e., wl→y(t) = "N"), agent l removes the ordered pair (l, y) from its RepuNet database Rl, thereby causing the network to evolve at time t. By leveraging gossip-driven reputation updates, we aim to ensure that agents can promptly update peer reputations, which prevents the stagnation of reputation updates due to a lack of further encounters.

## 4 Experiment

Our experiments aim to answer three key questions: (i) Does our RepuNet effectively avoid the 'tragedy of the commons'? (ii) How does our RepuNet prevent the occurrence of the tragedy? (iii) How effectively does each component of our RepuNet contribute to sustaining long-term cooperation? We conducted our experiments through two scenarios: (i) a voluntary participation task for reducing energy consumption, adapted from Erez et al. [53], which exemplifies a public goods dilemma between personal convenience and collective resource sustainability; and (ii) a trading investment task inspired by the trust game [4], highlighting a social dilemma between short-term self-interest and long-term cooperation in resource allocation. We outline the experimental settings in Section 4.1. We answer the first two questions in Section 4.2, and the last question through an ablation study in Section 4.3.

## 4.1 Experimental Settings

Scenario 1: Voluntary Participation We adapted the scenario presented by Erez et al. [53], which models a public goods dilemma in the real world. Agents decide whether to participate or not in a voluntary program to reduce energy consumption. The dilemma is that while participation lowers overall energy demand and benefits the collective, agents are often incentivized to not-participate for self-interest due to the inconvenience of reduced energy use. Each agent independently decided whether to participate and could revise its decision every five rounds of interactions (i.e., communications) with others. The simulation ran for 200 rounds and was repeated five times to ensure reliable and consistent results.

Scenario 2: Trading Investment Inspired by the trust game in an investment setting [4], we designed a sequential investment dilemma involving an investor and a trustee. The trustee proposed an allocation plan, and the investor decided whether to accept it. If accepted, the investor chose an amount to invest. The trustee then invested the funds, which were doubled, and decided whether to allocate them as agreed. Finally, both agents evaluated each other's behavior. The dilemma is that while adhering to agreements fosters cooperation, agents may be tempted to free-ride for short-term gains. Each agent started with 10 units of funds and was randomly assigned as an investor or a trustee in each round. Investors were then paired with trustees either randomly or through reputation-based selection, with equal probability. The simulation ran for 100 rounds and was repeated five times.

Controlled Settings To evaluate the effectiveness of RepuNet, we construct controlled settings for each scenario, where all agents adopt a standardized architecture commonly used in generative MASs [51, 28]. This basic agent architecture comprises three core modular components: profile, memory, and action. Specifically, the profile defines each agent's identity, encoding static attributes such as roles, preferences, and constraints that distinguish agents and guide decision-making. The memory module stores perceived or generated information across interactions, enabling agents to maintain behavioral consistency. The action module governs interactions with both the environment and other agents. The key difference between agents with and without RepuNet is that agents without RepuNet interact solely within the evolved network structure and lack the capability to evaluate peers, form reputations, or spread peer reputations through gossip.

Initialization We conducted experiments with 20 generative agents of diverse inclinations: some were prosocial, prioritizing collective interests, while others were self-interested, favoring individual gains. Details on agent descriptions are in Appendix C. For computational efficiency, we used GPT-4o mini in both scenarios. The GitHub repository for our project can be accessed via the following anonymous link: https://anonymous.4open.science/r/RepuNetB346.

## 4.3 Ablation Study on the Reputation System

We conducted an ablation study to answer the question of how effectively does each component of our RepuNet. Since RepuNet is an event-driven system reliant on direct social encounters within an embedded network, we focused on the impact of reputation at the agent level and the role of indirect gossip (Reputation and Gossip for short). Our ablation study included four conditions: (i) with RepuNet, (ii) without Gossip, (iii) without Reputation, and (iv) without RepuNet. In all cases, agents interacted within the network, and each experiment was repeated five times under identical settings.

As shown in Table 1, we averaged the performance over the final five rounds in both scenarios. RepuNet achieved the highest participation rate in Scenario 1 and the highest investment success rate in Scenario 2, confirming its effectiveness in preventing the 'tragedy of the commons'. We also note that removing gossip led to a slight decrease in performance, suggesting that direct encounters have a greater impact than indirect gossip. However, removing the Reputation or the entire RepuNet caused a sharp drop in rates (below 20%), suggesting that agents without RepuNet prioritized individual rationality, leading to non-cooperation and the 'tragedy of the commons'.

## 5 Conclusion

The study of reputation-based MASs has been a well-established area of AI for decades; on the other hand, generative AI technologies have recently captivated the world [37]. In this paper, we bridge these two fields together to establish a reputation-based generative MAS, sustaining cooperation and avoiding the 'tragedy of the commons'. Specifically, we proposed RepuNet, a novel reputation system operating at two levels: reputation dynamics at the agent level and network dynamics at the system level, both driven by direct encounters and indirect gossip. These dynamics, in turn, influence agents' behavior in future interactions. To evaluate RepuNet's effectiveness, we conducted two experimental scenarios: one assessing agents' cooperative behavior

in a public goods dilemma and another measuring their trustworthiness in a sequential investment dilemma. Extensive results show that reputation-enabled agents not only self-regulate their behavior effectively but also select reputable partners for cooperation, thereby preventing the 'tragedy of the commons'.

## 6 Limitations

Although we explored the effectiveness of reputation and emergent phenomena at both the agent and system levels, our study has certain limitations. Firstly, due to cost constraints, we did not examine the impact of multiple agents using different LLMs. Since LLMs are trained on distinct pre-trained datasets, agents powered by different models may develop varying reputations for the same behavior. Investigating this aspect could provide deeper insights into model-specific biases in reputation formation. Furthermore, our RepuNet focuses on individual reputation—both an agent's self-perception and its evaluation of others. However, in real-world scenarios, reputation is often more complex, as individuals can represent not only themselves but also their groups they belong to. Consequently, individual reputation can shape group reputation, and conversely, group reputation can influence individual reputation. We envision that reputation-based generative MASs would be a fruitful avenue for future research, and these limitations present some promising directions.

## D More Details of Indirect Gossip

Gossip involves the exchange of positive or negative information about absent individuals, making it an effective way to shape and spread reputations [13, 18]. Since gossip involves two or more agents in a conversation, we consider it from two perspectives: the gossiper's and the receiver's.

## D.1 The Gossiper's Perspective

In human societies, people have a strong tendency to gossip about others when they are (dis)satisfied with interactions, using it to influence others' reputations [13, 50]. While a good reputation is difficult to build, it can be easily damaged [49]. Inspired by these principles, we instruct each agent to evaluate its satisfaction with others' behavior after each interaction. This evaluation of satisfaction is based on the agent's own values and preferences, described in its agent descriptions D. Let m represent the retrieved memory of interactions with a target (such as a conversation or observation). The evaluating agent decides whether to gossip about the target's behavior, which can be represented by the LLM-based operation ywill ∈ {"Y", "N"} ← GossipW ill(m, D) . Agents who tend to gossip are referred to as gossipers. Once a gossiper is inclined to gossip (i.e., ywill = "Y"), it selects a listener based on its RepuNet database ϕ, which records relationships and reputations shaped through prior interactions. This process is represented by the LLM-based operation al ← GossipChoice(ϕ), where al denotes the selected listener.

## D.2 The Listener's Perspective

Gossip typically involves three agents: the gossiper, the gossiped-about target, and the listener. Therefore, we consider that when being involved in a gossip, a LLM-based agent (a listener) will identify the relationship and summarize information the gossiper intends to convey (or gossip information for short). Let G g→l represent a gossip between a gossiper and a listener. This LLM-based process can be represented as Ig Ig→l ← GossipIdentify(G g→l ), where Ig Ig→l is the summarized gossip information in natural language, such as "David raised concerns about Elena, her inconsistent performance led to a loss of trust..."

Inspired by human societies, people may exaggerate or misrepresent information to enhance their own reputation or harm others, thus it is essential for the listener to evaluate the credibility of summarized gossip information [7, 10, 21, 27]. Specifically, we prompt the listener to evaluate the credibility of this summarized gossip on a 5-point Likert scale, from "very uncredible" to "very credible". Inspired by Giardini et al. [17], the credibility of the gossip information is evaluated from two perspectives: (i) from an agent perspective, the credibility depends on the gossiper's reputation, with a higher reputation in the listener's perception indicating greater reliability; and (ii) from a community perspective, the credibility depends on listener's overall trust in prior gossip within the community, where a higher proportion of reliable gossip enhances the listener's trust in information shared through gossip. Let r g denote the gossiper's reputation, and P(I ¯ ) denote the proportion of prior gossip information rated "(very) uncredible" in the listener's memory. We represent this LLM-based operation by C(Ig Ig→l ) ← GossipEvaluate(Ig Ig→l, rg, P(I ¯ )). After evaluating the credibility, the listener stores the gossip information in its memory as a quadruple, θ =&lt; atar, a g, Ig Ig→l, C(Ig Ig→l ) &gt;. Here, atar and a g refer to the basic information of the gossiped target and the gossiper, respectively; Ig Ig→l is the summarized gossip information, and C(Ig Ig→l ) denotes its credibility rating.
